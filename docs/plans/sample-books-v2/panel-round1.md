## Reader 1 — Linda, 52, nurse in Ohio. Mother is 81 with early memory loss. Has looked at StoryWorth.

### Carolyn
- (a) 9 — I know her kitchen, her hours, her mother's recipe can, and what she does on a Saturday now; that's a life, not a résumé.
- (b) 8 — "Mom made a lot of gelatin salads" and "I was almost disappointed" are a real woman; "being scared and being brave happen at the same time" is the one line my mother would never say.
- (c) 9 — This is my mother's book: the table, the church she quit for a year, the daughter asking one question every Sunday; and the recordings matter to me more than the pages, because the voice is what goes first.
- (d) 7 — "Rick remembers it differently" is the best idea in here for a family with memory loss, it makes being wrong about the turkey a loving thing; but the letter's "There was a woman in Holland, Michigan, who loved..." reads like someone pre-writing her eulogy, and "the three best promotions I ever got" is a mug.
- (e) Best: "Mom's last loaf" — the rye held in her lap like a cat, and "I didn't want to remember it, and now I'm glad somebody asked." Cut: "Were you ever really scared?" — two paragraphs, one of them a moral, and the hospital story is better told in a sentence elsewhere.

### Raymond
- (a) 8 — I understand the shop and the marriage, and Elena better than Ray; what I don't know is what he was like at a Sunday dinner that wasn't about the company.
- (b) 7 — The caliper that reads a thousandth heavy is him; "The real test of building something is whether it runs without you" is a conference.
- (c) 5 — My mother never ran anything, and a book that is 70% payroll wouldn't fit her; but Elena's page, where she says what she had left after the mill, is the page I'd want from my mother.
- (d) 7 — The cover says "1983 – 2022" under his name and for a second I thought he'd died; otherwise the book stays out of its own way.
- (e) Best: "The Friday I couldn't make payroll" — Elena getting into the truck with the ledger. Cut: "Thursdays" — it's two memories stapled together, and the Maya mill story is already told better by Maya in the family chapter.

### Tom
- (a) 9 — Medic, night nurse, quiet father, the rosary and the picture; I know exactly which man this is, I've worked next to him.
- (b) 8 — "The nurses ran that hospital. Anybody who tells you different wasn't there at three in the morning" is word-for-word something I've said; "Silence protects you for a while. Then it costs the people who love you" sounds like it was written down first.
- (c) 8 — I'd want this for my father, not my mother; for her it's too heavy and the questions are built for a man with a war.
- (d) 6 — The front-matter line "Nothing on these pages was added that he did not say" is the company making a promise to me, in the book; and the yellow highlight on "come home" is somebody deciding for me which sentence to feel.
- (e) Best: "Mail call" with Diane's correction — forty-one versus forty-three, "you write a boy what you have." Cut: the "What Tom learned" spread — four sentences on two mostly empty pages, pulled out of stories that were better with the sentence left in.

## Reader 2 — Marcus, 44, software manager. Father is a retired contractor who "doesn't talk about himself."

### Carolyn
- (a) 8 — I get the bakery and the table; I'm less sure what she was like at work on a bad day, since every hard thing resolves by the next paragraph.
- (b) 7 — The turkey in the laundry basket with three family members disagreeing is unfakeable; "Happiness doesn't announce itself. It just looks like a normal Saturday" and then "this is it. Pay attention" twice is a writer closing a loop.
- (c) 6 — This is a book by a woman who likes talking, with a daughter who asks every Sunday; my dad would answer "fine" eleven times and this format would show that.
- (d) 6 — The sentence picked out as "kept" on each memory is the product telling me what the takeaway was, and the "What Carolyn learned" page does it again with headings like "Love" and "Work."
- (e) Best: "The Thanksgiving the oven died" — four people, four turkey weights, nobody yelled. Cut: "A good day, now" — it's a wrap-up, and it reuses the easel photo with a near-identical caption from six pages earlier.

### Raymond
- (a) 9 — Broom, lathe, Falcon, garage, pallet, ESOP; I could draw the timeline, and I know what he's like because I know how he hired.
- (b) 8 — "He showed me where my hand was wrong" and "I let it ring" are a machinist; "Start with the one thing you can do better than anybody. You learn the rest on the way" is a keynote.
- (c) 9 — This is the one that would work on my dad: every question is about work, and work is the only door he leaves open; "Was I scared? Every Friday" is an answer I could get out of him.
- (d) 6 — "Was I scared? Was I sure? What would I tell somebody who..." is the interview script bleeding through into his answer, and the ledger plate's "First order!" with an exclamation mark doesn't sound like Elena.
- (e) Best: "The customer who was sixty percent" — the trap with a nice paint job, and the rule named after him. Cut: "Hiring Hector" — the headnote promises Hector speaks at the end and he doesn't; fold the thumb-on-the-table into the next memory.

### Tom
- (a) 9 — Clearer than my own father's story, which is the point.
- (b) 7 — The Chevy started every Sunday and the restroom change in Detroit are real; but "polite is a closed door," "silence protects you for a while," "talking doesn't open the box," "everybody should have one person who asks twice" — four maxims in four memories reads like a ghostwriter who found his voice and then kept using it.
- (c) 8 — Hannah asking twice is the mechanism I'd actually need; I'd buy it to find out whether my father has a Hannah.
- (d) 5 — The highlight, the QR on the memory page, the quote wall, the last page's "The story keeps going" with a second QR, and the headnotes ("the longest pause on any of the recordings is on this one") — that last one is the company reviewing its own recordings inside the book.
- (e) Best: "The ’57 Chevy on Genesee Avenue" — one page, no moral, a father who said things by checking tires. Cut: "Telling it" — "I was a kid who did a hard job, then a man who did a useful one, and the two are connected" is the book summarising itself.

## Editor's notes (as myself)

### Carolyn Bennett — *Moments & Memories*

Strongest: the family corrections. Rick on the turkey weight, Dylan on nobody yelling, Ellie thinking it was a recipe — one event, four voices, and the book becomes a table instead of a monologue. The recipe plate and the grandchildren's two lines are the objects a buyer will photograph. Weakest: the last four pages. The letter's third-person self-portrait and the dedication's "three best promotions" are the only pages where an adult can hear a writer, and they are the pages read aloud at the funeral. Single change: cut the "There was a woman in Holland, Michigan..." paragraph and let the letter end on "The stories are already yours." The book then closes in her voice, not in the product's idea of her.

Flags:
- Written-for-her: "Everything important in my life got decided at a kitchen table" (epigraph + m1); "being scared and being brave happen at the same time. You don't get to wait for one to finish" (m11); "Happiness doesn't announce itself. It just looks like a normal Saturday" (m5) and the "this is it. Pay attention" callback repeated in m12; letter: "There was a woman in Holland, Michigan, who loved the people at her kitchen table more than she ever managed to say out loud. You're all of them. Pull up a chair."; dedication: "The three best promotions I ever got"; Dylan's family page: "Mom taught me that almost anything can be fixed with a warm kitchen and a little patience" (summary-speak); back cover "who always pulled up a chair" (product toast).
- Fact: m7 says "Every Saturday I take out the next card... in order" from Feb 2017, yet in Jan 2026 she is on card 212 of 340 — roughly 460 Saturdays have passed; at weekly cadence the can was empty by 2023. Either "most Saturdays" or a smaller number.
- Fact: m9 "I had fifty-seven years of her" — Ruth died Feb 2017; Carolyn (b. April 1960) was 56.
- Continuity: m1 headnote places the call "At the kitchen table on 17th Street," but m1 says the table "is in Dylan's kitchen now," and m6/m7 put Carolyn's own kitchen elsewhere (the R on her wall). Say whose kitchen.
- Minor: people list says Mark "Asked for a second slice"; Mark's page says Carolyn gave it unasked. People list "Tom, her brother — Brought nothing, as usual" is a joke with no referent in the book.
- Minor: m12 reuses the easel photo ("paintd") with a caption nearly identical to m4's.
- Child-safe: yes. No profanity, no gore.

### Raymond Ortiz — *The Ledger*

Strongest: Elena, in every voice but his. The deposit-slip meeting, the ledger on the dashboard, Marisol's "the seven's a one," and Elena's own page ("He thinks that page is about him") give the book a second subject and a spine. Tomás's "Five years, and I counted" is the best correction in any of the three books. Weakest: it is a founder's book first and a grandfather's second; the child and the nurse both dropped out, and the advice lines ("Start with the one thing...", "The real test of building something...", "The people are the company") come from a keynote, not a recliner. Single change: fix the cover. "1983 – 2022" under "Raymond & Elena" reads as a lifespan; two readers flinched. Use "BORN 1951 · SAN ANTONIO" like Tom's, and keep the company years on the ledger plate.

Flags:
- Written-for-him: "Start with the one thing you can do better than anybody. You learn the rest on the way" (m6); "angry lasts longer than sure" (m6); "It felt like winning. It was a trap with a nice paint job" (m8, borderline, in character); "If someone does the work of a partner, put their name on it the first day" (m4); "The real test of building something is whether it runs without you" (m11); letter: "The people are the company"; back cover: "Nobody builds anything alone, whatever the magazines say." The self-interview tics ("Was I scared? ... Was I sure? ... What would I tell somebody who...") expose the question list.
- Fact: m6 gives $6,000 savings + $5,000 loan, mill $2,300; Elena's page says the first ledger line "is about what I had left after it. Eighty dollars." Those numbers do not foot, and this is the one family where a reader will try (Marisol does).
- Fact/age: m11 dateline "March 2022 · aged 71" is right only if the Friday was on or after March 9. "Two years and four lawyers" for an ESOP decided mid-2019 and closed March 2022 is nearer three.
- Dangling: m7 headnote says Hector "does not say a word until the end" — there is no Hector side on m7. m10 "rule six" — the rules are never listed. Tomás "runs his own shop" (m8) is never explained against m5, where he wanted this one.
- Headnote voice: "the turn signal is on for most of it" is charming but is the company narrating its recording.
- Child-safe: yes, but ESOP / term sheet / private equity go unexplained; an 11-year-old loses the last two chapters.

### Thomas Kessler — *What I Carried Home*

Strongest: the physical record. The helmet calendar plate, the August 30 letter ("Tell me anything with snow in it"), Diane's forty-three versus his forty-one, Mike's silent bus ride. Dave scored it 10/9/10 and that reader is the buyer. Weakest: the maxims and the apparatus. Tom's voice carries more aphorisms per page than the other two — "polite is a closed door," "silence protects you," "talking doesn't open the box," "one person who asks twice" — and the "box" metaphor appears six times; a careful reader hears the shaping. On the page, the product is most visible here: highlighted kept-lines, two QR cards, a 2/3-empty "learned" spread, the colophon's "Nothing on these pages was added that he did not say," and the last-page archive pitch. Single change: cut "Telling it" page two ("I was a kid who did a hard job, then a man who did a useful one") and move "Little questions" off the Memorial Day spread.

Flags:
- Written-for-him: "Silence protects you for a while. Then it costs the people who love you" (m5); "Silence felt like strength. It was just silence" (m11); "Talking doesn't open the box. It just lets some air in" (m11); "What I want people to understand is simple. I was a kid who did a hard job, then a man who did a useful one, and the two are connected" (m11, summary-speak); "Everybody should have one person who asks twice" (m11); "Every person I helped in that ER was a small payment on what I couldn't do for Danny" (m10); "I've believed in staying every day since... Nobody should be alone at the end" (m4, tidy close). "Box" metaphor: m5 ×2, m11, letter ×2, Hannah's page.
- Product talking (rendered pages): the yellow highlight on the kept sentence (tom-memory.png); "Scan with a phone camera to hear Tom tell this" card on a memory page; the "What Tom learned" spread with four quotes and two-thirds white space (tom-learned.png); colophon lines "Nothing on these pages was added that he did not say" and "Shaped, designed and printed by A Story" (tom-colophon.png); final page "THE STORY KEEPS GOING... the family can keep adding to it" with QR (tom-final.png); title page "IN HIS OWN WORDS." Layout: "Little questions" cards face the Memorial Day page (tom-little.png). Headnotes such as "the longest pause on any of the recordings is on this one" are the company describing its own recordings.
- Fact check (all pass): Feb 9 1968 = ten days after Tết; calendar days 9/88/204/300/365 map to Feb 17 / May 6 / Aug 30 / Dec 4 / Feb 7 1969 correctly through the leap year; May 6 1968 was a Monday; Hannah 12 in 2016 → 16 in 2020 → matches her page; 47 years from 1969 to 2016; married 1971 → "fifty-five years" in 2026.
- Continuity (soft): m5 says Hannah "opened the box," but the box opens on the 2016 Honor Flight bus (Mike's side) and at the 2017 VFW, four years before Hannah's 2020 ask. Not a contradiction, but the book credits two different openings.
- Render: spread totals differ between screenshots (27 vs 28); check pagination before print.
- Repetition: "he keeps his coat on" appears in two headnotes (m1, m7).
- Child-safe: yes. No profanity, no gore; Danny's death is told without detail. It is heavy — the 11-year-old would read it with a parent — but nothing should be removed for a child.

## Cross-book notes

- Side-note bylines differ: Carolyn/Raymond use "Rick Bennett, her husband"; Tom uses "Remembered by Diane Kessler, his wife." Pick one before three books sit on the same website.
- Only Carolyn has photographs. Raymond has one plate and no images; on a website his sample will look thinner than the other two.
- Every book's (d) score is pulled down by the same five fixtures (kept highlight, QR cards, learned wall, colophon promise, last-page pitch). They cost roughly a point per book across the panel; the stories themselves barely registered as "product."
